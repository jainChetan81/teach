Five ways in

Lesson 0004 · ~30 min · completes hdr-6 · feeds capstone ps-5, ps-6

Why this lesson

You have five commands that all appear to do the same thing — get you onto the mini:

ssh chetanjain@192.168.68.101
ssh chetanjain@mini.bullhead-mine.ts.net
ssh chetanjain@mini
mosh chetanjain@mini
herdr --remote mini

They are not five alternatives. They are three different questions stacked on top of each other, and every one of those commands quietly answers all three. Once you see the three layers you will never again guess which one to type, and when one breaks you will know which layer to go and look at.

This matters for the capstone directly. "Move a live session between devices" is a sentence about all three layers at once.

Phase 1 — The whole idea in one table

In plain words first. Think of going to someone's house.

Address is which house. You can say "number 101 on my street", or "the Sharma house", or just "home" — three names, one building. Some names only work if you're already standing on that street.

Transport is which road you take, and roads behave differently. One of them collapses if you step off it for a second. The other lets you stop, change vehicles, and carry on.

Attachment is what's waiting when the door opens. Either an empty room, or your own desk with your work still spread out exactly as you left it.

Every one of your five commands picks one from each of those three. That's all this lesson is.
LayerThe question it answersYour options
1. AddressWhich machine, and how do I name it?192.168.68.101 · mini.bullhead-mine.ts.net · mini
2. TransportHow do the bytes actually travel?ssh (TCP) · mosh (UDP)
3. AttachmentWhat do I land in — a shell, or a session?a plain shell · herdr

Read your five commands again with that in hand. ssh chetanjain@192.168.68.101 is LAN address + SSH transport + plain shell. herdr --remote mini is alias address + SSH transport + herdr session. Nothing else is going on.

The one sentence to keep: the layers do not compete, they stack. Picking Tailscale does not mean giving up mosh. Picking mosh does not mean giving up herdr. The best setup uses one from each row.

Phase 2 — Addressing: three names, one machine

192.168.68.101 — the LAN address

Handed out by your home router, by DHCP. Two consequences that bite:

  • It only works on your home Wi-Fi. Step outside, it is meaningless.
  • It can change. Reboot the router, add devices, and the mini may come back as .104. Your muscle memory is then wrong and the error looks like the machine is down.

mini.bullhead-mine.ts.net — the MagicDNS name

bullhead-mine.ts.net is your tailnet. Every device you have joined gets a name inside it — you currently have mini, macbook, contabo-vps and iphone. The name resolves to a 100.x.x.x address, which is not a public internet address and not a LAN address: it exists only inside your Tailscale network.

This is the one that works from a café, from cellular, from anywhere both machines are logged into Tailscale. It is the address layer the capstone depends on.

mini — the short name

This is the interesting one, because it can be true for two completely different reasons, and you should know which one you are relying on:

  1. A Host mini block in ~/.ssh/config on the MacBook, which rewrites it to the full name.
  2. MagicDNS adding bullhead-mine.ts.net as a DNS search domain, so a bare mini resolves on its own.

Reason 1 only helps ssh-family tools. Reason 2 helps everything on the machine. ssh -G mini tells you which is in play. On this mini, ~/.ssh/config already does exactly this for the other direction:

Host macbook
    HostName macbook.bullhead-mine.ts.net
    User hackerearth467
    IdentityFile ~/.ssh/id_ed25519_tailscale
    IdentitiesOnly yes
    ServerAliveInterval 30
    ServerAliveCountMax 6
Note the User line, and note that it is not your name. The MacBook's account is hackerearth467; the mini's is chetanjain; the VPS is chetan. This is exactly why a typo'd mosh chetjain@mini fails with a confusing permission error rather than a helpful one. A Host block with User set means you can stop typing the username at all — just ssh mini. Deleting the habit of typing usernames and IPs is literally an objective on your networking track.

Phase 3 — Transport: what mosh actually is

This is the layer you said you don't understand, and it is the one with the most surprising design.

In plain words first.

SSH is a phone call. One line, held open the whole time. Everything you say goes down that one line. Walk out of range and the call drops — and the conversation is simply over. You don't get it back, you start again.

Mosh is texting. Nothing is held open. Each little message stands on its own, and the thread is remembered by who you are, not by which tower you were near. So you can go from Wi-Fi to phone data mid-sentence and the thread just carries on.

And here is the bit that surprises everyone: mosh still uses SSH — but only to unlock the door. It calls once, lets its own program in, then hangs up the phone and switches to texting. That's why mosh needs your SSH keys to work, and also why it stops caring about the SSH connection a second later.

SSH is one TCP connection, and your session is that connection

Open it, and there is a socket between the two machines. Everything you type goes down it. If that socket dies — you change networks, the Wi-Fi drops, the laptop sleeps long enough — the session dies with it, and whatever was running in it usually dies too. ServerAliveInterval 30 in the config above does not prevent that; it just makes the death happen promptly and visibly rather than leaving you staring at a frozen terminal.

Mosh uses SSH once, then throws it away

Here is the part almost nobody knows. When you run mosh mini:

  1. It SSHes in normally, using your keys and your ~/.ssh/config. That is the whole authentication story — mosh has no login system of its own.
  2. Over that SSH connection it runs mosh-server on the mini, which prints a session key and a UDP port.
  3. It closes the SSH connection entirely.
  4. From then on your terminal talks to mosh-server over UDP (typically ports 60000–61000), using mosh's own protocol.

That design buys three things:

  • It survives your IP changing. The session is identified by a key, not by an address. Walk from Wi-Fi to cellular mid-command and it just carries on. TCP cannot do this.
  • It survives sleep. Shut the lid, open it hours later, it reconnects.
  • Typing feels instant. Mosh predicts and echoes your keystrokes locally, showing unconfirmed ones underlined. On a laggy connection this is the difference between usable and miserable.

And it costs you real things:

  • No port forwarding. -L and -R do not exist in mosh. If you need a tunnel, you need SSH.
  • No agent forwarding, no X11, no scp. Mosh is for interactive terminals only.
  • UDP must get through. Some hotel and corporate networks block it. SSH's single TCP port is far more likely to survive a hostile network.
  • Its scrollback is not yours. Mosh keeps only a screen; scrollback belongs to your terminal app.
  • mosh-server must be findable on the remote machine's PATH — in a non-interactive shell. That is the .zprofile trap from lesson 0003, and it breaks mosh for exactly the same reason it breaks herdr --remote.
Mosh itself is free and open source. It is installed here at /opt/homebrew/bin/mosh via Homebrew, no licence, no account. If something in your setup is asking you to pay, that is the terminal app you run mosh inside — not mosh. Worth separating those two before you blame the wrong thing.

Phase 4 — Attachment: a shell is not a session

The first four commands all drop you into a shell. A shell is a child of the connection. When the connection goes, the shell goes, and its children usually go with it.

herdr --remote mini is a different kind of thing. It uses SSH underneath — which is exactly why the PATH trap killed it — but what it hands you is not a fresh shell. It is a view onto the session the mini's herdr server is already holding. The PTYs live on the mini and belong to the server. Your client is disposable. That is the whole thesis of lesson 0001, and this is where it becomes a command you type.

Two things fall straight out of that table. First: mosh and herdr solve different halves of the same problem. Mosh keeps the connection alive across a network change; herdr keeps the work alive across a client death. Neither one does the other's job. Together they cover everything in the first three rows.

Second: nothing saves you from the last row, and you already know why from lesson 0003 — the herdr server is a LaunchAgent that needs a login, and FileVault parks the mini at a password screen after any reboot. That is a genuine hole in the capstone, not an oversight, and it is the reason a VPS keeps looking attractive.

Retire ssh chetanjain@192.168.68.101 from your fingers. It is the only one of the five that stops working the moment you leave the house, and it is the only one that can silently start pointing at a different machine.

Check yourself (instant feedback)

1. Mosh survives you switching from Wi-Fi to cellular. Why can't SSH do that?

2. What does mosh use SSH for?

3. You're on mosh, running an agent in a plain shell (no herdr). You close the terminal window by accident. What happened to the agent?

4. ssh mini works from your MacBook. Which layer is doing the work of turning mini into a real address?

Done with this lesson when

You can name the three layers without notes; you can explain in one sentence what mosh uses SSH for and why it then stops using it; and you can say what mosh protects that herdr does not, and what herdr protects that mosh does not. Then tick hdr-6 on the herdr plan.

Open questions to settle on your own machine

  • Your mosh is the plain open-source one, not a paid app. There is a separate App Store product called Moshi that speaks the mosh protocol and charges for it. You are not using it. Your mosh is free, open source, and already on the MacBook — so if something misbehaves, it is a mosh problem, not a licence problem. Write down what misbehaves; "it has issues" is not something a future you can debug. First suspect: mosh-server not on the non-interactive PATH.
  • The phone leg does not exist yet. You cannot run mosh on iOS without some app in between. Reaching the mini from the iPhone is a separate decision, not a thing you already have.
  • Does mosh reach the mini from outside your home network? Careful here, because you have half the answer and it is easy to over-read. From the office, on mobile internet, you reached the Contabo VPS with both ssh and herdr. That is a genuine result and it settles more than it looks: a foreign network, real internet in between, and herdr --remote working across it. But it is still the easy direction — the VPS sits in a datacentre and is trivially reachable from anywhere. The untested direction is inbound — you, on some café Wi-Fi, reaching the mini which sits behind your home router's NAT. That is the one Tailscale has to work for, possibly by falling back to a DERP relay, and it is the same gap as capstone ps-6. Do not tick it off the VPS result.

    Worth noticing though: if the VPS becomes your always-on herdr host instead of the mini, this test never needs to happen. It also sidesteps the FileVault reboot hole from lesson 0003 completely. You may have already proven the setup that matters.

Go deeper (primary sources)

  • man ssh_config — Host, HostName, User, IdentityFile, ServerAliveInterval. The five directives that cover almost everything you do.
