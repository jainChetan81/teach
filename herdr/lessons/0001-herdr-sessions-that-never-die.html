Herdr: sessions that never die

Lesson 0001 · ~30 min · feeds net-4 → net-6 and capstone ps-1

Why this lesson, why first

Every later lesson in the networking track assumes you've felt a persistent session: an agent keeps working while your laptop lid is closed, and you reattach from another device like nothing happened. Herdr gives you that feeling in 10 minutes. Phase 1 is hands-on usage (including from your phone). Phase 2 is how it works — because the whole portable-sessions capstone is about porting this exact trick to t3code.

Phase 1 — Use it

Do every step. Don't read ahead — the "how" lands harder after the "wow".

Phone can't reach your Mac? That's not a herdr problem — it's the exact gap Tailscale fills (your devices on one private network, no port forwarding). It's queued in net-7. For today, same-Wi-Fi is fine.

Phase 2 — How it works

Read this once, slowly. It's the architecture you'll be re-deriving for t3code later.

your terminal (thin client) your phone (thin client) │ │ │ Unix socket SSH ─────────┘ ▼ ▼ ┌───────────────────────────────────────────────────┐ │ herdr session server (always on) │ │ │ │ owns the REAL PTYs: pane 1 ▸ claude (working) │ │ pane 2 ▸ shell │ │ tracks state: blocked / working / done / idle │ └───────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

Three ideas carry everything:

1. The server owns the session, not your terminal. When you "run herdr", you're starting (or attaching to) a background process that holds the actual pseudo-terminals your agents live in. Your terminal window is a disposable view. Closing it kills nothing — same reason a dead browser tab doesn't kill a website.

2. Clients are thin and interchangeable. The TUI talks to the server over a local Unix socket. Over SSH, your local herdr acts as a thin client streaming the remote server's UI (herdr --remote) — or you just SSH in and run herdr there. Mac, phone, future VPS: all just clients. This is the formula the capstone ports to t3code: state lives in one long-lived place; devices are interchangeable windows onto it.

3. Mosh and herdr solve different layers. Herdr keeps your session alive when the connection dies. Mosh keeps your connection alive when the network changes (UDP, roaming, rekeying). Stacked together: mosh holds the pipe, herdr holds the work. You can lose both ends and nothing dies.
